2(a) Elicitation, negotiation and presentation

Stakeholders. Direct: the customer (a module lecturer representing the University who may market and sell the game) and players; the customer described the target player as a 16-year-old student interested in computer science. Indirect: the ENG1 team that will take over, maintain and extend our project in Assessment 2.

Elicitation. We used two main elicitation techniques [1, 4]: (1) Document analysis, reading through the product brief and assessment paper to extract initial requirements and separate reduced Assessment 1 scope from full Assessment 2 scope; and (2) a semi-structured customer interview, preparing specific questions beforehand to clear up vague points in the brief while leaving room for follow-up questions. We considered user questionnaires and workplace observation [1], but ruled out questionnaires because a single customer sets the project brief, and ruled out observation because this is a new game with no existing system in use. Key points from the interview were:

QuestionCustomer answerRequirements affected
What counts as a hidden event? A triggered activity that changes the game state; "hidden" means the player does not know its consequences in advance; the story must stay consistent; no marks for creativity alone. FR_EVENT_HIDDEN, FR_EVENT_TRIGGER, FR_EVENT_EFFECT
How should the geese behave? They should stop the player moving freely ("bullet hell" style crowding pressure). FR_GEESE_SPAWN, FR_GEESE_BLOCK
Are multiple difficulties needed? The game must be easy to play and short; a harder difficulty is optional. UR_CASUAL, QR_LEARNABILITY
Any graphics preference? 2D top-down is fine. CR_2D_TOPDOWN
Who is the target audience? A 16-year-old student interested in computer science. UR_CASUAL, QR_LEARNABILITY, QR_MAP_CLARITY
Where on campus should it be? An open campus area with recognisable University features. UR_CAMPUS_MAP, FR_CAMPUS_THEME

Negotiation. Different interpretations within the team were resolved against the brief and customer answers [1, 4]:

  • Keeping scope realistic (KISS): Extra difficulty modes were left out of mandatory scope once the customer confirmed they were optional, avoiding unrequested complexity; speed and count values are kept in one place for tuning.
  • Turning subjective goals into testable targets: Informal terms ("fun", "casual", "winnable") were turned into measurable Volere fit criteria [3] (QR_LEARNABILITY, QR_MAP_CLARITY, QR_BALANCE) to be checked during the Assessment 2 user evaluation. The win threshold in FR_WIN_CONDITION is left configurable for play-testing.
  • Splitting A1 and A2 clearly: Every system requirement is tagged as A1, A2, or split A1 / A2 to match the reduced Assessment 1 brief.

Presentation. Our requirements layout follows the ENG1 requirements lecture [4], Sommerville [1] and ISO/IEC/IEEE 29148 [2]:

  • Two levels of detail: User requirements (UR_) state high-level goals in plain English for the customer; system requirements (FR_, QR_, CR_) state testable developer obligations without locking in class or method names too early [1, 4].
  • Readable, stable IDs: Topic-based prefixes (UR_, FR_, QR_, CR_) do not break if rows are added or reordered, making it easy to reference the same IDs in UML diagrams, Java comments and tests [2, 4].
  • Fit criteria and priorities: Every system requirement links back to its user requirement(s), every quality requirement includes a measurable fit criterion [3], and priorities use standard IEEE terms: Shall (mandatory) and Should (desirable) [2].
  • Tables rather than long use-case forms: We considered full text and UML use-case templates [1, 4] but chose compact tables because continuous real-time game rules are clearer to read and cross-reference within the 4-page limit.

2(b) User and system requirements

Overall product need: A short, casual, single-player 2D game in which a University of York student escapes a flock of hungry geese on campus within 5 minutes by using items sensibly and dealing with hidden events.
