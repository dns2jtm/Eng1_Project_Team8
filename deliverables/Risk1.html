5(a) Risk management process and risk register format

Risk categories and management process

Following Sommerville [1] and the ENG1 risk lecture [2], we grouped candidate risks across four main types so technical, team and commercial stakeholder issues were all covered: Project (schedule, people, coordination), Product (game quality, performance, libraries), Project & Product (overlapping schedule and quality impact), and Business (viability of the product for the customer who intends to market the game, plus Assessment 2 handover).

Our team followed a four-step iterative risk management process [1, 3] across Weeks 2–7:

  1. Risk identification: First, we brainstormed potential instabilities and failure points that could arise over a 6-week group project, covering people availability, misunderstood requirements, Git integration, toolchain compatibility and third-party licensing.
  2. Risk analysis (simple qualitative scales): Because ENG1 develops a small non-critical desktop game with no historical failure dataset, numeric probability percentages would be artificial [2]. Following the assessment guidance, we rated Likelihood and Severity (Impact) on simple three-level scales (Low, Medium, High):
    RatingLikelihoodSeverity (impact if the risk occurs)
    LowUnlikely during the 6-week schedule.Minor rework (≤0.5 day); absorbed without affecting deliverables.
    MediumPlausible in a 7-student coursework team.Delays a task by 1–3 days or degrades non-critical polish.
    HighLikely to happen without coordination.Threatens submission deadlines, page limits, runnable JAR or marks.
  3. Risk planning (mitigation and damage control): Following [1, 3] and Section 4.8, many risks (illness, clashing deadlines, OS/JVM differences) cannot simply be "prevented". For each risk we recorded practical mitigation and fallback steps that minimise how much the project is harmed if the issue occurs (e.g. task shadowing for bus factor, side-by-side review branches before merging, isolating OpenGL in screen/, and keeping all balance values in GameConfig).
  4. Risk monitoring: Throughout the project, Elliott and Dawud reviewed the register alongside our weekly plan snapshots (planning.html) so emerging issues (such as Week 6 branch integration and JAR licence bundling) were caught early and mitigated before wider repercussions developed.

Justification of risk register format

  • Risk ID and Type: Stable IDs (R01–R11) allow cross-referencing from weekly plan snapshots, while Sommerville's four types [1] check breadth beyond code bugs.
  • Description & project effect: States both the trigger event and its consequence on deliverables or requirements (Req1 IDs), justifying the Severity rating [2].
  • Colour-coded Likelihood and Severity (Low, Medium, High): Keeps prioritisation fast and readable during weekly team check-ins without over-engineered spreadsheets.
  • Mitigation method and named Ownership: Focuses on concrete impact-reduction actions and assigns named individuals (never "everyone") so accountability is clear [2].

Monitoring summary at Assessment 1 submission

Active weekly monitoring caught four risks when they arose in Weeks 5–6: R04 (two prototype branches reconciled cleanly via review/side-by-side), R06 (transitive LGPL audio libraries documented with notices bundled inside GoosePatrol.jar), R09 (0.05 s simulation sub-stepping added), and R11 (standalone cover pages replaced by compact headers and URLs updated when repository ownership moved to dns2jtm). All mitigations worked without delaying submission.
